The Matchbox Educable Noughts And Crosses Engine — Donald Michie's bead-and-cardboard learner, rebuilt as a live simulation.

MENACE only ever plays ◯ and always moves first. Behind the board sits a drawer of matchboxes — one per position it might face — each holding coloured beads standing for the moves it could make. Shake a box, tip out a bead, play the square that colour names. Win, and the beads that led there get reinforced; lose, and they get pulled from the box for good. Play a few games yourself, or set it training against a mindless or a perfect opponent and watch the drawer fill up.

      History & mechanismHow MENACE works

      In 1961 the British researcher Donald Michie built a noughts-and-crosses player out of matchboxes and a supply of coloured beads, one box for each essentially different position it could face: 287 of them by the count in his 1963 paper, though most later accounts give 304. He called it MENACE: the Matchbox Educable Noughts And Crosses Engine. During the Second World War Michie had worked at Bletchley Park on the German “Tunny” teleprinter cipher, first in the Testery and then in Max Newman’s Newmanry alongside Jack Good, and his contact there with Alan Turing left him with a lasting interest in machines that learn.

      Each matchbox stood for one board position MENACE might face on its own turn — it always moved first, playing ◯. Rotations and mirror images of the same position share a single box, which is how the game collapses to a few hundred cases instead of many more. Inside each box sat coloured beads, one colour for each move MENACE could make there; whichever colour the operator shook out named MENACE’s move.

      How many colours does a box need? In Michie’s own scheme, described in his 1963 paper, moves that are mirror images of each other within a box share one colour: the empty board needs only three, for a corner, an edge and the centre. A simpler scheme gives every empty square its own colour, so the empty board needs nine. Both play the same game, but Michie’s pools the beads of equivalent moves, so each colour is reinforced more often. This page uses Michie’s scheme unless you switch the Bead rule control to one colour per square.

      Learning was simple bookkeeping. Win, and three more beads of the colour just drawn went into every box used that game — success bred confidence. Draw, and each of those boxes gained a single bead. Lose, and the drawn bead was removed for good, making that move a little less likely next time. Boxes for the opening move started with four beads of each colour, boxes for MENACE’s third move with three, its fifth with two and its seventh — the last move it had any real choice about — with one, so a bad habit late in the game could be trained out almost immediately.

      Starve a box of every bead and MENACE had nothing left to draw: it resigned. In a tournament Michie played against it in 1961, MENACE was drawing consistently after about twenty games. Michie called it “a trial-and-error device which learns to play”: an early working example of a machine learning from success and failure, in the way now called reinforcement learning. In 1965 Michie became director of the University of Edinburgh’s Experimental Programming Unit, which grew into its Department of Machine Intelligence and Perception, formed in 1966. With his colleague Roger Chambers he later carried the matchbox idea over to BOXES (1968), a program that learned to balance a pole on a moving cart.

      This page runs Michie’s rules against a virtual jar of beads: the same 4/3/2/1 starting beads, the same +3/+1/−1 bookkeeping, and the same resignation when a box runs dry. Its drawer is built differently from his. It has a box for every position in which MENACE has a real choice: 304 in all, the figure most accounts give, where his 1963 paper counts 287. And his machine was a fixed set of matchboxes glued together like a chest of drawers, each box marked with a drawing of its position; here, each box is opened the first time MENACE actually reaches that position, so the drawer fills up as you play. With the Michie 1963 bead rule, the colours are shared just as in his paper.
