MENACE

The Matchbox Educable Noughts And Crosses Engine — Donald Michie's bead-and-cardboard learner, rebuilt as a live simulation.

MENACE only ever plays ◯ and always moves first. Behind the board sits a drawer of matchboxes — one per position it might face — each holding coloured beads standing for the moves it could make. Shake a box, tip out a bead, play the square that colour names. Win, and the beads that led there get reinforced; lose, and they get pulled from the box for good. Play a few games yourself, or set it training against a mindless or a perfect opponent and watch the drawer fill up.

History & mechanismHow MENACE works

In 1961, the British AI researcher Donald Michie — a former Bletchley Park codebreaker who spent the Second World War in the Testery, working alongside Alan Turing and Jack Good on German teleprinter ciphers — built a noughts-and-crosses player out of 304 empty matchboxes and a jar of coloured glass beads, together with his colleague Roger Chambers. He called it MENACE: the Matchbox Educable Noughts And Crosses Engine.

Each matchbox stood for one board position MENACE might face on its own turn — it always moved first, playing ◯. Rotations and mirror images of the same position were sorted into a single box, which is how the game collapses to 304 cases instead of many more. Inside each box sat beads in nine colours, one per empty square; whichever colour the operator shook out named MENACE's move.

Learning was simple bookkeeping. Win, and three more beads of the colour just drawn went into every box used that game — success bred confidence. Draw, and each of those boxes gained a single bead. Lose, and the drawn bead was removed for good, making that move a little less likely next time. Boxes for the opening move started with four beads per colour; by the seventh move — the last one MENACE had any real choice about — each colour was down to a single bead, so a bad habit late in the game could be trained out almost immediately.

Starve a box of every coloured bead and MENACE had nothing left to draw: it resigned. Play it against a sharp opponent for long enough, though, and it stopped losing altogether — one of the earliest working demonstrations that a machine could improve at a task purely through trial, error, and reward, years before "reinforcement learning" was standard vocabulary. Michie went on in 1965 to found the University of Edinburgh's Department of Machine Intelligence and Perception.

This page runs the same rules against a virtual jar of beads: 304 boxes waiting to be discovered, the same 4/3/2/1 starting beads, the same +3/+1/−1 bookkeeping, and the same resignation when a box runs dry. Michie pre-built every box in advance; here, each one is opened the first time MENACE actually reaches that position, so the drawer fills up as you play.
